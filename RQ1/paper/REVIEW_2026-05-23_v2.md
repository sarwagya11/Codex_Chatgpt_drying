### R2. Domain reviewer

**Carried over from v1:** W1 (geographic-only novelty), W2 (kinetic uncertainty propagation not closed), W3 (elevation inconsistency), W4 (season list mismatch), W5 (Aktaş diffusivity over-precise), W6 (single kinetic dataset). Status updates below.

**Status updates.**

- v1 W3 (elevation numbers): **partially shifted, not resolved.** Table 6 in §5.3 lists site elevations as 72 m (Biratnagar), 1350 m (Kathmandu), 1550 m (Dhulikhel), 1820 m (Taplejung). These now agree with §1¶4 ("72 to 1820 m elevation"). But §4.2 still reports the same sites at 80 m, 1302 m, 1482 m, 1783 m. So the inconsistency has not been removed; it has been relocated. **§4.2 is now the deviant section** and a reviewer who reads in section order will encounter the §4.2 numbers first, then find different numbers in §5.3 Table 6. Three sections of the same paper should not give three different elevations for the same site (and they should match the simulator). Fix once at §4.2 to align with §1¶4 and §5.3.

- v1 W4 (season list): **unchanged.** §1¶4 still says "monsoon, winter, autumn, spring"; §4.2 still says "autumn, winter, spring, summer" with the latter described as "pre-monsoon irradiance peak". No monsoon window appears in the simulation matrix.

**New weakness from §5.3.**

- W7. **Taplejung warmer than Kathmandu in Table 6 is counter-intuitive and goes uncommented.** *Problem:* Table 6 reports Taplejung (1820 m) at 16.9 °C annual mean vs Kathmandu (1350 m) at 15.7 °C. Naive lapse-rate reasoning expects Taplejung to be cooler, not warmer, by ~3 K. The §5.3 text uses this fact ("Taplejung has a warmer annual mean than Kathmandu") but does not explain why a higher-elevation site is warmer in the TMY record. *Why it matters:* Readers familiar with the Himalayan-mid-hill climate will read the table as a TMY artefact or a coordinate error and lose trust unless the manuscript pre-empts the question. The most likely explanation is microclimatic (Taplejung valley vs Kathmandu valley + urban-heat-island absence at Taplejung), but this needs to be stated. *Suggestion:* Add one sentence after Table 6 noting that the inversion is consistent with the PVGIS-SARAH3 TMY for these grid cells and reflects valley-microclimate effects rather than a coordinate error. Cross-check the coordinates against a Nepal gazetteer. *Severity:* Major.

### R3. Perspective reviewer

**Carried over from v1:** W1 (no economic dimension), W2 (deployment scale undeclared), W3 (no grid-availability discussion), W4 (monsoon advertised in §1 but missing from §4.2).

**Status updates.**

- v1 W4 (monsoon): **still unresolved.** The new §5.3 does discuss site-to-site irradiance penalties at Kathmandu and Taplejung, which is the right material for a monsoon discussion, but neither §5.2 nor §5.3 explicitly addresses the July-September monsoon window. If apples are not dried in monsoon, §1¶4 should drop "monsoon" from the season list and §5.3 should state the seasonal calendar explicitly.

**New strength.**

- S3. The climate-driver decomposition in §5.3 is exactly the kind of analysis that translates the four-site sweep into a transferable design statement. The mechanism (the HRX dampens climate sensitivity because exhaust enthalpy is most valuable in cold humid air) is the seed of a Nepal-specific design recommendation. Build on it in §6 by translating into a one-sentence siting rule (e.g., "below 1.5 km elevation, the Solar contribution dominates; above 1.5 km the HRX dominates"). The data in Table 7 already support this and a sentence would carry the practical claim.

**New weakness.**

- W5. **The Kathmandu/Biratnagar ratio is used as the climate-sensitivity proxy without justification.** *Problem:* §5.3 uses the ratio of Kathmandu SEC to Biratnagar SEC as the headline climate-sensitivity number (last column of Table 7) and discusses 58-84% penalties for the B family. This is one of several equally defensible proxies (max/min across all four sites; coefficient of variation; mean penalty against Biratnagar). Without explaining the choice, the reader cannot judge whether Kathmandu is the right denominator. *Why it matters:* Kathmandu is the *worst* site at every family except the resistive baseline, so Kathmandu/Biratnagar overstates climate sensitivity relative to a less extreme pair. *Suggestion:* Either justify the choice ("the Kathmandu/Biratnagar pair brackets the operating envelope") in §5.3 or report both Kathmandu/Biratnagar and the four-site coefficient of variation, and use whichever cleanly supports the narrative. *Severity:* Minor.

### R4. Devil's Advocate

**Carried over from v1:** DA1 to DA7 (topology robustness, kinetic uncertainty propagation, fixed-schedule comparison, geographic novelty, transferability, ε coupling, fan-class invariance). Status updates below.

**Status updates.**

- DA1 (ε/ΔT_pinch robustness): **unchanged.** The new T_set sweep tests a *different* sensitivity axis. The headline ranking remains a single point in (ε, ΔT_pinch).
- DA2 (kinetic uncertainty propagation): **unchanged.** As noted under R0 and R2, the new T_set sweep is sensitivity, not propagation.
- DA3 (fixed-schedule framing): **partially improved.** The new §5.2 explicitly shows drying time falls from 14.2 to 10.1 h as T_set climbs, which makes the "fixed-schedule" framing of §5.1 more visible by contrast. §5.1 still does not state "this is an energy-cost ranking at fixed throughput", but the new §5.2 demonstration of variable throughput at variable T_set makes the v1 weakness less acute. A one-sentence cross-reference would close it.

**New issue from the additions.**

- DA8. **The new §5.3 Table 7 includes D3 with no caveat, and the discussion ignores D3.** *Problem:* If D3 was added because reviewer/author thought a third HRX variant was needed for completeness, it should be defined and discussed; if D3 was a code-already-supported configuration included only for thoroughness, the manuscript should say so and either (a) include D3 in the comparative narrative, or (b) flag D3 as an exploratory case outside the main comparison and explain why D3's climate sensitivity is anomalously low. As drafted, D3 appears, gets ranked, and is silently excluded from the §5.3 narrative on the HP + HRX family ("D1 and D2 each carry a 26-36% Kathmandu penalty" with no mention of D3's 13%). This is a worst-of-both-worlds presentation. *Severity:* Critical (overlaps with R1 W7).

- DA9. **Set-point sweep doesn't include a lower bound at 40 °C.** *Problem:* The kinetic dataset spans 40-50 °C. §5.2 sweeps 45-50-55 °C, with 55 °C as a deliberate extrapolation. A 40 °C case would lie *within* the kinetic-dataset band, would test whether the E2 advantage holds at the lower thermolabile-product-friendly end, and would close the operator-relevant T_set range from below. Without it, the sweep tests only "the same or hotter than baseline", not "the same or colder". *Why it matters:* For thermolabile products (apple aroma fractions degrade above ~55 °C; see §1¶2 framing), a 40 °C operating point is the practically interesting one. *Suggestion:* Add a T_set = 40 °C row per site for E2 and E3 in Table 5. The simulator already has the kinetic engine fit at this temperature. *Severity:* Major.

- DA10. **Re-running E2 at 50 and 55 °C raises a downstream sizing question that goes unstated.** *Problem:* Table 5 shows E2 T_cond at 65 °C (within the 70 °C envelope cap in §3.2 assumption xii) and reports the result as if no envelope flag fired. If T_cond at 65 °C is genuinely never out-of-envelope on any TMY hour, fine; but the manuscript would benefit from a sentence confirming this for the 55 °C case. *Severity:* Minor.
